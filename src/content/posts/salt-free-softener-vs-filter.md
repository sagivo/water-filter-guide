"Do I need a filter or a softener?" is one of the most common — and most confused — questions in home water treatment. The confusion is understandable: they're sold side by side, sometimes in the same cabinet, but they do fundamentally different jobs.

## Two problems, two technologies

**Water filters remove contaminants.** Activated carbon, catalytic carbon, KDF, and carbon block media target chlorine, chloramines, lead, mercury, VOCs, PFAS, sediment, and hydrogen sulfide. Every system in our [12-system roundup](/posts/best-whole-house-water-filters-2026/) is a filter — they make water cleaner and better-tasting.

**Softeners and conditioners address hardness.** Hard water contains dissolved calcium and magnesium. It won't make you sick, but it forms scale inside pipes and water heaters, leaves spots on dishes, makes soap lather poorly, and shortens appliance life. No filter removes these minerals — that's a separate job.

Many homes have both problems: chlorinated city water that's also hard, or well water with iron plus hardness. Those homes need both technologies, installed in series (filter first, then softener/conditioner, typically).

## What "salt-free softener" really means

The term is marketing shorthand, and it's misleading. Salt-free systems don't soften water at all — they **condition** it. The dominant technology is TAC (template-assisted crystallization), sold under brand names like NaturSoft:

1. Hard water flows through TAC media.
2. Dissolved calcium converts into microscopic, stable crystals.
3. The crystals flow through plumbing without sticking to surfaces as scale.

The result: dramatically less scale buildup, but a hardness test afterward still reads "hard." The minerals are still there; they just don't form scale. The Pelican PSE2000's NaturSoft media was tested at 99.6% scale prevention — strong evidence for what it claims, as long as you understand the claim.

Salt-free conditioners need almost no maintenance (no salt bags, no regeneration cycles, no brine discharge) and add no sodium to the water. Their limitation: in very hard water, or where you want that slippery "soft water" feel, they don't deliver what ion exchange does.

## Salt-based ion exchange: the real softener

Traditional softeners use ion exchange resin: calcium and magnesium ions swap places with sodium ions. The hardness minerals are genuinely removed — water tests soft afterward, soap lathers richly, and scale stops forming.

The trade-offs: regular salt refills, water used in regeneration cycles, brine discharge (restricted in some municipalities), and added sodium in the water (a consideration for sodium-restricted diets and some plants). For very hard well water, though, ion exchange remains the proven answer.

## Installation order matters

When you run both technologies, sequence them correctly: sediment pre-filter first, then the main contaminant filter, then the softener or conditioner last. The filter protects the softener's resin or TAC media from sediment and chlorine (chlorine degrades ion-exchange resin over time), and the conditioner sees the cleanest water. Reversing the order shortens the life of the more expensive downstream stages. Combo cabinets like the Pelican PSE2000 handle this sequencing internally.

Test your water. If the report shows contaminants (chlorine, lead, PFAS) but low hardness, buy a filter from our [roundup](/posts/best-whole-house-water-filters-2026/) and stop there.
