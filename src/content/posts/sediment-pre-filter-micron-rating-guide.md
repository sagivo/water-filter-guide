The sediment pre-filter is the cheapest, least glamorous part of a whole-house system — and the one that determines whether the expensive parts survive. Its job is simple: catch sand, silt, rust flakes, and scale **before** they reach your carbon filter, softener, or fixtures. Get the micron rating wrong and you either clog the pre-filter in weeks (too fine) or let grit through to foul everything downstream (too coarse). Get it right and a $15–$35 cartridge quietly protects thousands of dollars of equipment.

This guide covers what micron ratings actually mean, how to pick between 5 and 20 micron, the staging sequences plumbers use, and the sizing mistakes that starve homes of water pressure.

## What a micron rating actually measures

A micron is one-millionth of a meter — about 1/25,000 of an inch. A filter's micron rating is the **particle size it reliably traps**. To calibrate your intuition, here are real-world particle sizes:

| Particle | Typical size (microns) |
|---|---|
| Human hair | ~70 |
| Fine beach sand | 60–200 |
| Pollen | 10–100 |
| Red blood cell | ~6–8 |
| Giardia / Cryptosporidium cysts | ~4–6 |
| Most bacteria | 0.5–5 |
| Viruses | ~0.02–0.3 |
| Dissolved salts (what makes water "hard") | <0.001 |

Two things fall out of this table immediately. First, a 20-micron filter stops sand, rust flakes, and large silt — the visible stuff. Second, **no sediment filter touches dissolved contaminants** — hardness minerals, lead, fluoride, and nitrates are thousands of times smaller than the finest sediment pore. If your [water test](/tools/water-test-guide/) shows dissolved problems, sediment filtration is step zero of the treatment train, not the treatment itself.

One more critical distinction: **nominal vs. absolute ratings.** A *nominal* micron rating means the filter catches roughly 85% of particles at the stated size — the other 15% pass through. An *absolute* rating means it catches 99.9% or more at that size. Absolute is the stricter, more honest number. Two cartridges both labeled "5 micron" can perform very differently if one is nominal and the other absolute, so check the spec sheet rather than trusting the box. For pre-filters, nominal ratings are common and fine — you're catching bulk grit, not polishing drinking water.

## The standard ratings and where each belongs

Whole-house sediment filtration uses a small set of standard ratings, each with a defined role:

- **100 micron (spin-down):** catches visible sand and coarse grit. Flushable designs clean with a valve twist — nearly zero running cost. First stage for sandy well water.
- **50 micron (spin-down or cartridge):** coarse pre-screening; protects valves and downstream cartridges from heavy sediment loads.
- **20 micron (cartridge):** the standard municipal-water pre-filter. Catches rust flakes, pipe scale, and debris from main breaks without meaningful flow restriction. The default pick when you're unsure.
- **5 micron (cartridge):** the standard well-water pre-filter, or second stage after a 20-micron. Clears visible sediment, fine silt, and sand; protects carbon blocks and softener resin downstream.
- **1 micron (cartridge):** polishing stage. Catches fine silt and many bacteria-sized particles — used ahead of UV systems (sediment shadows bacteria from UV light) or as final whole-house polishing.
- **0.5 micron and below:** carbon-block and specialty territory — cyst reduction, fine chemical polishing. Not a pre-filter job.

The pattern: **ratings step down along the water's path.** Each stage catches what the last one let through, and each stage is protected by the coarser one before it. A 5-micron cartridge fed by raw well water full of sand dies in weeks; the same cartridge fed by a 50-micron spin-down lasts months.

## 5 micron vs 20 micron: the real decision

This is the question behind most sediment-filter shopping, and the answer depends on your water source:

**Choose 20 micron when:** you're on municipal water with normal sediment levels. City water's grit is mostly rust flakes and pipe scale — 20 micron catches it all, maintains near-full flow, and the cartridge lasts 6–12 months. It's also the right first cartridge stage in a two-stage setup: 20 first, 5 second.

**Choose 5 micron when:** you're on well water with visible sediment, fine silt, or iron flakes — or when you're protecting fine downstream media (a carbon block, a softener, a UV system) and want the extra polishing. The cost: a 5-micron cartridge clogs faster and creates more pressure drop as it loads, so budget for more frequent changes — sometimes every 3–6 months on gritty wells.

**The mistake in both directions:** going 5 micron on clean city water buys you nothing but shorter cartridge life and earlier pressure drop. Going 20 micron (or nothing) on a sandy well lets fine grit through to score softener resin, blind carbon beds, and clog fixture aerators — damage that costs hundreds to fix. Match the rating to the sediment load, which your water test and a look at your current faucet aerators will tell you.

## Staging: the order water should meet your filters

Professional installs follow a deliberate sequence — plumbers call it staging — and the order is non-negotiable:

1. **Coarse sediment first** (spin-down 50–100 micron, or 20-micron cartridge on city water)
2. **Fine sediment second** (5-micron cartridge on well water)
3. **Iron/manganese treatment** if needed (oxidation or dedicated media — see our [iron and manganese guide](/posts/well-water-iron-manganese-guide/))
4. **Carbon filtration** (chlorine, taste, odor, chemicals)
5. **Softener/conditioner** if needed
6. **UV disinfection** last if needed (needs clear water to work — sediment ahead of UV is mandatory)

**Why this order matters:** sediment always goes first because it's the cheapest stage to replace and the one that protects everything else. Put carbon before sediment and the carbon cartridge becomes a $40–$80 sediment filter that exhausts in weeks instead of years — the single most common DIY staging error. Reverse-stage systems (carbon first, marketed as "enhanced adsorption") are mechanically flawed: by the time water reaches the sediment stage, the carbon is already fouled and its chlorine-removal capacity is gone.

## Cartridge types: spun, string-wound, and pleated

Not all sediment cartridges with the same micron rating behave the same:

- **Spun (melt-blown) polypropylene:** the standard disposable. Fibers get denser toward the core, so they trap progressively finer particles through their depth. Cheap ($15–$35 for Big Blue sizes), widely available, and the default for most pre-filters. Replace, don't clean.
- **String-wound:** polypropylene or cotton string wound around a core. Good dirt-holding capacity and slightly more tolerant of heavy sediment loads than spun. A solid pick for gritty well water as a first cartridge stage.
- **Pleated (washable):** polyester or cellulose with a large pleated surface area. The standout feature is washability — rinse and reuse several times, which cuts running costs on moderate-sediment water. The [Kind E-1000](/filters/kind-e1000/) in our guide ships with a washable 20-inch pleated sediment filter for exactly this reason, and the APEC CB3 pairs one with KDF media. Pleated cartridges also flow better for their rating thanks to the extra surface area.

For most homeowners: spun polypropylene in a standard housing is the simple, correct choice. Upgrade to pleated when you're changing cartridges often enough that washability pays back, or string-wound when the well throws heavy grit.

## Sizing the housing: don't starve your pressure

A sediment filter that's too small for the household is worse than a coarse rating — it chokes flow. The rules:

- **Housing size:** Big Blue 20" x 4.5" housings are the whole-house standard for a reason — the large cartridge surface area keeps pressure drop low even as the filter loads. Slim 10" x 2.5" housings belong under sinks, not on main lines.
- **Port size:** 1-inch inlet/outlet ports match standard residential plumbing and maintain 15 GPM without meaningful restriction. Undersized ports are a hidden bottleneck no micron rating can fix.
- **Peak GPM:** size for your household's peak demand, not average use. Our [flow sizer tool](/tools/flow-sizer/) calculates it from bathrooms and fixtures; our [sizing guide](/posts/what-size-whole-house-water-filter-do-i-need/) covers the full method.
- **Pressure gauges:** install one before and one after the sediment housing. Normal residential pressure runs 45–80 psi (around 60 ideal). When the downstream gauge reads 10–15 psi lower than the upstream one, the cartridge is clogging — change it. Gauges turn replacement from a calendar guess into a measurement, and they're the feature [Express Water's Heavy Metal system](/filters/express-water-heavy-metal/) gets right with a gauge on every stage.

A properly staged, properly sized sediment setup maintains full household flow for months. A clogged or undersized one drops the whole house to a trickle — and homeowners invariably blame the carbon filter or the softener, when the $20 pre-filter was the culprit all along.

## The bottom line

Sediment filtration is the foundation everything else stands on: 20 micron for city water, 5 micron (usually staged behind something coarser) for well water, spin-down first when there's sand, and a housing sized to your peak GPM with gauges to tell you when to change the cartridge. It's the least expensive stage in the system and the one that makes every downstream dollar — the carbon tank, the softener, the [replacement schedule](/posts/how-often-to-change-whole-house-water-filter/) — actually pay off. Spend the $15–$35 and the half hour on install order, and the rest of your system gets to do its real job.
