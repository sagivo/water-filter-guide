If you're on city water and your tap water smells like a swimming pool, there's a good chance your utility disinfects with chloramine, not chlorine — and that one detail quietly disqualifies most whole-house water filters. Standard activated carbon, the hero media in most city-water systems, removes chlorine beautifully and chloramine barely at all: real-world whole-house filters test under 20% chloramine reduction with plain carbon. Buy the standard system for chloraminated water and you've spent four figures to fix taste while the actual disinfectant sails through.

This guide covers what chloramine is, why ordinary filters miss it, the one media that actually works, and how to buy a system that handles your city's water specifically.

## What chloramine is and why cities use it

Chloramine is free chlorine chemically bonded to ammonia. Water utilities create it on-site — it's not a product you can buy — because it holds its disinfecting power much longer than plain chlorine. In a long distribution network, free chlorine decays before it reaches the far ends of the system; chloramine survives the trip. That's the entire reason it's there: reliability at the tap, miles from the treatment plant.

The trade-off is that chloramine is harder to remove by design. It's stable, it doesn't off-gas, and it laughs at the trick that works for chlorine — let a pitcher sit in the fridge overnight. A chloraminated pitcher sits there tomorrow tasting like chlorine-plus-ammonia. And yes, fish owners know this well: chloramine is toxic to fish and amphibians, which is why aquarium shops sell dechlorinators rated specifically for chloramine. Your water utility is required to tell you which disinfectant it uses, and it's the first thing to confirm before shopping.

## Why standard carbon fails on chloramine

Activated carbon removes chlorine through adsorption and reduction reactions on the carbon surface — water flows past, chlorine sticks and reacts, clean water exits. It's fast and it works at whole-house flow rates with a modest tank. Chloramine's chemical structure needs a more aggressive reaction. Plain granular activated carbon (coconut shell or coal-based, either way) does an excellent job on free chlorine but does not remove chloramine — and it doesn't even strip the chlorine out of the chloramine molecule, leaving ammonia behind.

The practical numbers make the point: standard carbon pulls under 20% of chloramine in real filters. So a "whole house water filter" that doesn't name catalytic carbon is, for chloraminated water, a very expensive placebo for your main complaint.

## Catalytic carbon: how the actual fix works

Catalytic activated carbon is carbon that has been specially manufactured — typically through a high-temperature gas treatment that alters its surface structure — to act as a catalyst. Instead of merely adsorbing chloramine, the modified surface promotes a rapid redox reaction that breaks the chloramine bond and reduces it to harmless chloride. The compound is destroyed, not just trapped, which is also why catalytic carbon beds last longer than plain carbon beds attempting the same job.

Key properties to understand before you buy:

- **It's a different product, not a marketing word.** "Carbon" and "catalytic carbon" on a spec sheet are different media. Look for the literal words "catalytic carbon" — coconut-shell based products like Calgon's Centaur are the common reference, and maker claims cite catalytic activity up to 10x that of regular activated carbon.
- **It handles chlorine too.** Catalytic carbon isn't a chloramine-only specialist; it removes chlorine, hydrogen sulfide (rotten egg smell), and VOCs as well. If your city ever switches back to chlorine seasonally — some utilities alternate — the same media covers both.
- **Flow rate matters more than usual.** Chloramine needs more contact time than chlorine. A tank that handles 10 GPM for chlorine may only be rated for a lower effective flow for chloramine. Size by the maker's chloramine guidance, and when in doubt buy one size up. Our [GPM sizing guide](/posts/what-size-whole-house-water-filter-do-i-need/) walks through the math.

## Step one: confirm what your city actually uses

Do not guess. Your utility's annual Consumer Confidence Report names the disinfectant — search your utility's website or the EPA's database for it. Alternatively, call your water utility and ask. This takes five minutes and it decides the entire purchase:

- **Chlorine:** standard activated carbon is fine. Pick from our [2026 roundup of the best whole-house systems](/posts/best-whole-house-water-filters-2026/).
- **Chloramine:** you need catalytic carbon. Continue below.
- **Mix or seasonal switch:** buy catalytic carbon anyway — it handles both.

## What to look for in a chloramine-capable system

**Named catalytic carbon in the spec sheet.** Not "advanced carbon," not "premium carbon" — the words "catalytic carbon." This is the single most important line on the page.

**Tank volume matched to your home.** More media means more contact time. A 1-cubic-foot tank in a household of four typically needs media replacement within 4–5 years on chloraminated water; larger tanks with million-gallon ratings stretch much further. Match the tank to your bathroom count, not the marketing headline flow rate.

**A sediment pre-filter.** Chloramine media fouls when sediment hits it first. Both architectures work; they fail differently:

| | Tank (backwashing/upflow) | Cartridge (Big Blue / 3-stage) |
|---|---|---|
| Media life | Years (million-gallon ratings) | 6–12 months per cartridge set |
| Upfront cost | Higher (~$1,100+) | Lower (~$400–$650) |
| Maintenance | Pre-filter swaps only, eventual media replacement | Regular cartridge changes, 1–2 hours DIY |
| Flow handling | Better at high flow (contact time built in) | Watch pressure drop on large homes |
| Best for | Long-term city-water homes | Budget installs, rentals, moderate water use |

For chloramine specifically, tanks win on contact time — the failure mode to avoid is an undersized cartridge choking flow and letting chloramine through as the media loads. If you go cartridge, buy the catalytic-carbon cartridge explicitly and track replacement by gallons, not by calendar.

## Don't forget the shower and the RO pre-filter

Two often-missed spots where chloramine matters:

- **Showers.** Hot shower spray volatilizes chloramine, and it dries skin and hair. A whole-house system fixes every shower at once — that's half the comfort argument. Point-of-use shower filters use vitamin C or small carbon blocks; they work for a single shower but can't match point-of-entry coverage.
- **Reverse osmosis systems.** RO membranes are damaged by chloramine. Every under-sink RO on chloraminated water needs a catalytic carbon pre-filter to protect the membrane — check your RO's pre-filter spec. This is the one place where "whole-house" and "drinking water" treatments intersect mechanically.

## Red flags in chloramine filter marketing

- **"Removes chlorine and chloramine!" with plain GAC in the spec sheet.** Without catalytic carbon, the chloramine claim has no mechanism.
- **No flow rate attached to the claim.** Chloramine removal at 2 GPM through a lab bench is not the same as at your home's 8–12 GPM service flow. Honest makers name both.
- **Whole-house RO for chloramine alone.** RO membranes don't remove chloramine without catalytic pre-filtration anyway, so a whole-house RO pitched as the chloramine fix is either misconfigured or massively oversold.
- **"Chlorine" used as a synonym for "chloramine."** Sloppy specs that never say which disinfectant they tested against are guessing. Ask for the test conditions.
