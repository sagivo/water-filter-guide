City water and well water are different filtration problems. City water arrives disinfected — your job is removing chlorine, chloramines, and the disinfection byproducts. Well water arrives raw: whatever the aquifer carries comes straight to your tap. Buy a city-water carbon tank for an iron problem and you'll learn this the expensive way.

## The usual well water suspects

**Iron** — the most common well water complaint. It stains fixtures and laundry orange, gives water a metallic taste, and feeds iron bacteria that form slimy deposits. Even moderate iron fouls standard carbon media, which is why city-water tanks underperform on wells.

**Manganese** — iron's frequent companion. It causes black or brown staining and a bitter taste, and standard filters handle it poorly without the right media or oxidation.

**Hydrogen sulfide (H2S)** — the rotten-egg smell. It's a dissolved gas, so it needs KDF media, aeration, or oxidation — a plain carbon tank will disappoint you.

**Sediment** — sand, silt, and rust particles that clog fixtures and wear out appliances. A sediment pre-filter is cheap insurance on any well system.

None of these are reliably addressed by the carbon-plus-KDF tanks that dominate city-water roundups. Our [how-to-choose framework](/posts/how-to-choose-whole-house-water-filter/) covers the decision steps; this guide covers the well-specific hardware.

## Don't skip the sediment pre-filter

Whatever main system you choose, well water deserves a dedicated sediment pre-filter upstream of it. Sand and silt chew through expensive KDF and carbon cartridges fast — a $30–$50 washable or replaceable sediment filter protects the stages downstream and is the cheapest maintenance you'll do. The APEC CB3 builds a sediment stage in; if you go with a tank system on a well, add one separately. Check it monthly for the first quarter; you'll quickly learn your well's sediment load and can set a replacement schedule from there.

## pH matters more than you'd think

Low pH (acidic water, below ~7.0) is common in wells and causes two problems: it corrodes copper plumbing (blue-green stains are the tell), and it makes iron and manganese harder to filter. If your lab report shows pH under 7, a calcite neutralizer stage belongs ahead of your iron treatment. It's an unglamorous add-on that the filter guides often skip, but acidic water quietly undermines everything downstream of it.

## When you need a dedicated iron filter or softener

Cartridge systems have limits. General guidance from manufacturer specs and owner reports:

- **Iron above ~3 ppm or manganese above ~0.5 ppm:** consider a dedicated iron filter (oxidation-based) rather than stretching a KDF cartridge system.
- **Hardness above ~7 gpg with visible scale:** a softener or conditioner belongs in the chain — filters don't remove hardness.
- **H2S so strong you smell it from the next room:** aeration or chemical oxidation may be needed before filtration.

A lab test quantifies all of this. The $150 test that tells you your iron is 1.2 ppm versus 8 ppm is the difference between a $650 cartridge system and a $2,000 dedicated iron filter — buy the test first, the hardware second. One more well-specific tip: retest every few years or after any change in taste, smell, or staining, since aquifer chemistry shifts over time and a system sized for last decade's water may be undersized for today's.
