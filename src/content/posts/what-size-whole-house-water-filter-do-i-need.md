Buy the wrong size and you'll know it every morning: someone showers while the dishwasher runs and the water pressure falls off a cliff. Sizing a whole house filter isn't about the size of your house — it's about the maximum gallons per minute your household can draw at once. Get that number right and everything else follows.

## The GPM chart: start here

| Residents | 1–2 baths | 2–3 baths | 3–4 baths | 4–5 baths |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | 5 GPM | 7 GPM | 10 GPM | 12 GPM |
| 3–4 | 7 GPM | 10 GPM | 12 GPM | 14 GPM |
| 5–6 | 10 GPM | 12 GPM | 14 GPM | 18 GPM |

This widely used chart (via Pure Water Gazette's sizing guide) is a starting point, not a prescription. Your real number comes from your fixtures — which brings us to the better method.

## The fixture method: calculate your peak demand

Add up what could realistically run at the same time on a busy morning:

| Fixture | Typical draw |
|---|---|
| Shower | 2–2.5 GPM |
| Bathtub filling | 3–4 GPM |
| Toilet flush | 2–3 GPM |
| Dishwasher | 1.5–2.5 GPM |
| Washing machine | 2–3 GPM |
| Kitchen faucet | 1.5–2 GPM |
| Outdoor spigot / irrigation | 3–5 GPM |

Two showers plus a dishwasher and a flushing toilet: roughly 9–11 GPM. Now **add ~20% headroom** — a filter rated at exactly your peak runs at its pressure limit in real use, and the resulting pressure drop shows up as the weak-shower complaint that drives most undersized-system returns. So 11 GPM of demand means shopping for 12–14 GPM of rated capacity.

## Port size: the bottleneck nobody checks

Here's the trap: you buy a 15 GPM system, but its inlet/outlet ports are 3/4-inch while your main line is 1-inch. The filter becomes a restriction point no matter what its GPM rating claims. **Match the ports to your main line** — most modern homes run 3/4-inch or 1-inch mains. Adapters exist, but downsizing costs flow, which defeats the purpose of sizing correctly in the first place.

## Why bigger (within reason) filters better

Flow rate isn't the only thing size buys you:

- **Contact time.** Water spends more time in contact with the media in a larger tank, which improves contaminant removal — especially for chlorine, chloramine, and PFAS where adsorption needs dwell time.
- **Capacity.** More media means more gallons before exhaustion. A 20-inch Big Blue housing outlasts a 10-inch one on the same water, and tank systems measured in hundreds of thousands of gallons barely notice a busy household.
- **Pressure stability.** An oversized filter barely registers normal demand, so pressure stays consistent even at peak.

## Special cases that change the math

- **Irrigation.** If sprinklers run off the same main, either size for them or — better — plumb irrigation to bypass the filter. There's no reason to filter lawn water.
- **Well pumps.** Size to the pump's output too; a filter can't deliver more than the well provides, and an oversized filter on a weak well just adds pressure loss.
- **Backwashing systems.** A backwash cycle can draw 75–120 gallons per cycle that needs somewhere to go — relevant on septic. Factor the drain, not just the GPM.
- **Future additions.** Planning a bathroom remodel or an ADU? Size for it now. Upsizing later means rebuying the system.

## The 30-second version

Count bathrooms, estimate simultaneous fixtures, add 20%, match your pipe size, and don't filter the sprinklers. Do that and you'll never think about sizing again — which is exactly how it should be. ## Worked example: sizing a 4-bed, 3-bath home

The Hendersons (hypothetical, but typical): 4 people, 3 bathrooms, dishwasher, washing machine, no irrigation on the house main.

Busy-morning worst case: two showers (2.5 + 2.5), dishwasher (2), one toilet flush (2.5) = **9.5 GPM**. Add 20% headroom → **~11.5 GPM target**. A 12 GPM system like the [SpringWell CF4](/filters/springwell-cf4/) fits cleanly; a 9 GPM [CF1](/filters/springwell-cf1/) would be right at the edge — fine on normal mornings, noticeable when everything runs at once.

Now change one variable: add teenagers who shower simultaneously while laundry runs. Peak jumps to ~13 GPM → target ~15.5 GPM → now you're in [Kind E-1000](/filters/kind-e1000/) / [iSpring WGB32B](/filters/ispring-wgb32b/) territory at 15 GPM. Same house, different household, different answer. This is why the fixture method beats the bathroom-count chart — it captures how *your* family actually uses water.

## Tank vs cartridge sizing: what changes

**Cartridge systems** are modular: need more flow? Add another Big Blue housing in parallel. Two 20-inch housings in parallel roughly double the flow capacity and the media volume. This makes cartridge systems forgiving to size — when in doubt, add a housing.

**Tank systems** come in fixed sizes (9, 12, 20 GPM and the like). You pick the tank that covers your peak plus headroom. You can't add half a tank later, so the 20% rule matters more here — and so does honest assessment of your peak. The upside: a correctly sized tank needs almost no maintenance for years, while an oversized cartridge system just means fewer cartridge changes.

## Don't forget the water heater and irrigation

Two fixtures people leave out of the math:

- **The water heater** doesn't draw GPM the way a shower does, but a tankless/on-demand heater has a minimum flow activation and a maximum it can heat. An undersized filter starving a tankless heater produces the classic symptom: hot water that surges hot-cold-hot. If you have tankless, size generously.
- **Irrigation zones** draw 3–5+ GPM each and run for long cycles. If they're on the house main, either include them in peak demand or — strongly preferred — plumb a dedicated irrigation line that bypasses the filter entirely. Filtering thousands of gallons of lawn water exhausts media for zero benefit.

## When to call it: the decision checklist

- [ ] Peak simultaneous demand calculated (fixtures, not just bathrooms)
- [ ] 20% headroom added
- [ ] Main line diameter measured (3/4" or 1")
- [ ] Filter port size matches the main line
- [ ] Irrigation bypassed or accounted for
- [ ] Tankless heater needs considered
- [ ] Drain + outlet available if choosing a backwashing tank
