PFAS — per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances — are the contaminants behind the "forever chemicals" headlines. Thousands of these synthetic compounds have been used for decades in nonstick cookware, stain-resistant fabrics, waterproofing, and firefighting foam. Their defining trait is persistence: the carbon-fluorine bond is one of the strongest in chemistry, so PFAS don't break down in the environment or in the human body. They accumulate.

## Why PFOA and PFOS get the attention

PFOA (perfluorooctanoic acid) and PFOS (perfluorooctane sulfonate) are the two most studied PFAS compounds. Research has linked long-term exposure to a range of health concerns, which is why they've become the focus of drinking-water regulation. The EPA has established health advisories and moved toward enforceable standards for these compounds — the regulatory details have evolved, so check current EPA guidance rather than older summaries.

Where does PFAS in tap water come from? Industrial discharge, firefighting foam runoff near airports and military bases, wastewater, and landfill leachate. Concentrations vary enormously by location, which is why step one is always the same: [test your water](/tools/water-test-guide/) before spending money.

## Whole-house vs under-sink: a scope decision

For drinking-water-only protection, under-sink reverse osmosis (certified to NSF/ANSI 58) is the most proven and cheapest PFAS removal technology. But drinking is only part of exposure. PFAS can be absorbed through the skin and inhaled in shower steam, and they deposit on laundry and dishes.

A whole-house PFAS system addresses the full exposure picture — every tap, every shower, the washing machine. The trade-off is cost: whole-house systems run $1,000–$2,100+ versus a few hundred for under-sink RO. Many households with confirmed PFAS contamination run both: whole-house for general reduction, under-sink RO for drinking water. Neither approach is wrong; it depends on your test results and budget.

## How PFAS removal actually works in these systems

Three technologies show up in residential PFAS treatment, and it helps to know which one you're buying:

**Granular activated carbon (GAC)** — the workhorse. PFAS compounds adsorb onto carbon surfaces, with longer-chain compounds (PFOA, PFOS) captured more readily than short-chain ones. The Aquasana HealthShield's certification is built on this. GAC works, but the media exhausts — which is why the HealthShield's 165,000-gallon capacity and ~$260/year filter costs are the price of certified performance.

**Catalytic carbon plus KDF** — the SpringWell CF1's approach. Catalytic carbon has enhanced adsorption properties versus standard GAC, and the maker reports PFAS reduction in its testing. Credible as a secondary benefit; not a substitute for certified PFAS treatment if that's your primary concern.

**Reverse osmosis (point-of-use)** — the most proven residential PFAS removal, certified under NSF/ANSI 58, but only at a single tap. Whole-house RO exists but is expensive, wasteful, and overkill for most homes.

Ion exchange resins are a fourth option used in commercial treatment, but they rarely appear in residential whole-house systems at research time.

## The honest testing advice

PFAS-specific lab testing costs more than a standard water panel, but it's the only way to know whether you need a PFAS system at all. Many city water supplies test below levels of concern; some wells near industrial sites or airports test far above. Don't buy a $2,100 PFAS system on the basis of a headline about a different zip code.

Once you have results, the decision framework is simple: confirmed PFAS at levels that concern you, go with the strongest certification you can afford (HealthShield).
