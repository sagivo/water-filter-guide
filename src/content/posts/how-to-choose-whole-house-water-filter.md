Picking a whole house water filter is a matching problem, not a ranking problem. The "best" system for chlorinated city water can be nearly useless for iron-heavy well water. This five-step framework — built from manufacturer specs, published reviews, and owner reports — gets you to the right system without overbuying or under-sizing.

## Step 1: Test your water first

Everything downstream depends on knowing what's in your water. City water utilities publish annual consumer confidence reports, but those describe the distribution system average — not what reaches your tap. Well water is unregulated, so testing is on you.

A lab test (typically ~$100–$200) covers the contaminants that matter:

- **City water:** chlorine or chloramines, lead, VOCs, PFAS, pH, hardness.
- **Well water:** iron, manganese, hydrogen sulfide (rotten-egg smell), sediment, hardness, pH, plus bacteria screening.

Test strips are cheap and fine for a quick chlorine or pH check, but they can't quantify iron or detect PFAS. See our [water test guide](/tools/water-test-guide/) for kit options and how to read a lab report. If you skip this step, you're guessing — and a $1,500 carbon tank won't fix an iron problem.

## Step 2: Size by flow rate (GPM) and bathrooms

Flow rate is the first sizing gate. When demand exceeds a system's rated GPM, water gets less contact time with the media and pressure drops at the tap — the classic symptom is a shower that weakens when the washing machine runs.

Rough guidance for peak household demand:

- **1–2 bathrooms:** 9–12 GPM covers most homes.
- **3–4 bathrooms:** 12–15 GPM.
- **4+ bathrooms, irrigation, or large multi-story homes:** 15+ GPM or a high-flow system.

Our [flow sizer tool](/tools/flow-sizer/) walks through fixture counts and simultaneous use to estimate peak demand. Note that rated GPM and peak GPM differ — the Aquasana Rhino EQ-1000, for example, rates 7 GPM with a 14.6 peak — so read the spec sheet carefully before comparing systems side by side.

## Step 3: Read the certifications, not the marketing

Water filtration marketing is full of impressive-sounding claims. Certifications from NSF International or the Water Quality Association (WQA) are what separate tested performance from adjectives.

- **NSF/ANSI 42** — aesthetic effects: chlorine taste and odor. Good, but not a health claim.
- **NSF/ANSI 53** — health-related contaminants: lead, cysts, certain VOCs.
- **NSF/ANSI 401** — emerging contaminants: some PFAS compounds, pharmaceuticals.
- **NSF/ANSI 58** — reverse osmosis systems (point-of-use, not whole-house).
- **NSF/ANSI 61 / 372** — component safety and lead-free materials, not contaminant removal.

Watch for the fine print: "NSF/ANSI 42/53 components" means parts of the system meet the standard, which is not the same as whole-system certification. The Aquasana HealthShield's WQA certification to NSF/ANSI 53 + 401 is a whole-system claim — that's why it's our top PFAS pick in our [PFAS guide](/posts/pfas-forever-chemicals-filter-guide/). Maker-tested or maker-claimed results are better than nothing, but independent certification is the stronger evidence.

## Step 4: Choose tank vs cartridge

Two architectures dominate the market:

**Tank systems** (SpringWell CF1, Aquasana Rhino, Pelican PSE2000) use large media beds with capacities of ~1 million gallons. Upfront cost is higher (~$1,000–$3,000), but upkeep runs ~$35–$120/year and media lasts 6–10+ years. They're the set-and-forget choice for city water.

**Cartridge systems** (iSpring WGB32B, Express Water, APEC CB3) use Big Blue filter housings with replaceable cartridges. Sticker price is lower (~$420–$700), but cartridges need replacing every 6–12 months at ~$126–$220/year. They shine for well water — KDF85 cartridges handle iron and H2S — and for budget buyers who accept the ongoing maintenance. See our [well water iron and manganese guide](/posts/well-water-iron-manganese-guide/) if that sounds like your situation.

## Step 5: Compare 5-year cost, not sticker price

A $450 system with $150/year cartridges costs more over five years than a $1,150 system with $35/year upkeep. Quick math on two real examples from our [12-system roundup](/posts/best-whole-house-water-filters-2026/):

- **iSpring WGB32B:** ~$450 + 5 × $126 ≈ **$1,080**
- **SpringWell CF1:** ~$1,150 + 5 × $35 ≈ **$1,325**

Closer than the sticker prices suggest — and the CF1 pulls ahead further after year six. Our [cost calculator](/tools/cost-calculator/) does this comparison for any two systems, including installation estimates.

## Installation: what to expect

Most whole-house systems install at the main water line where it enters the home, which usually means a plumber — budget roughly $200–$500 for professional installation depending on your area and how accessible the main line is. Tank systems like the SpringWell CF1 and Rhino EQ-1000 are floor-standing units that need vertical clearance and a drain nearby for backwashing models. Wall-mount cartridge systems like the Kind E-1000 are the friendliest DIY installs in the category, with many owners reporting a straightforward Saturday project. Whatever you buy, add a bypass valve and pressure gauges on either side of the system: the bypass lets you service the unit without shutting off the house, and the gauges tell you when cartridges are clogging.

## Putting it together

Test first, size by flow, demand real certifications, pick the architecture that fits your water and maintenance appetite, and compare total cost of ownership.
