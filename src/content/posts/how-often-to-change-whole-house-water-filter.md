The box says "change every 6 months." Your neighbor's well-water cartridge clogged in five weeks. A tank system owner hasn't touched the media in four years. All three are telling the truth — because a whole house filter's replacement schedule depends on the media type, your water, and how much of it you use.

## The short answer: replacement intervals by media type

| Media / component | Typical interval | Change sooner when |
|---|---|---|
| Sediment pre-filter cartridge | 3–6 months | Well water, visible particles, pressure drops |
| Carbon block cartridge | 6–12 months | Chlorine taste or odor returns |
| Granular activated carbon (GAC) | 6–12 months | Odor returns or flow slows |
| KDF media | 12–18 months | Heavy chlorine/chloramine load |
| Tank media (backwashing systems) | 5–10 years | Iron/sulfur load exhausts it early |
| UV lamp (if fitted) | 12 months | Output fades before the lamp visibly dims |
| Softener resin | 10–15 years | Chlorinated water or iron fouling |

These are starting points from manufacturer guidance and installer experience — not guarantees. A two-person home on clear municipal water may stretch a carbon cartridge to a full year. A family of five on a private well can clog a sediment cartridge in a month. Your water writes the real schedule.

## Why the calendar is the worst way to decide

A calendar reminder assumes average water, average use, average everything. Your home isn't average. The pros' method is dead simple: **watch the pressure drop across the system.**

Install a pressure gauge on each side of the filter housing (many systems have gauge ports built in). When the difference between inlet and outlet pressure grows noticeably, the cartridge is loading up — regardless of what the calendar says. Reduced flow at fixtures is the same signal without the gauges: if showers weaken house-wide but the municipal pressure is fine, the filter is the bottleneck.

This matters because sediment clogs filters long before their chemical capacity runs out. A carbon cartridge rated for 100,000 gallons can choke on sediment at 30,000 if your water is gritty. The gauge catches that; the calendar doesn't.

## The signs you're already overdue

You don't have to wait for a reminder. Systems usually announce themselves:

- **Pressure drop house-wide** — the single most common sign of a loaded sediment cartridge.
- **Chlorine taste or odor returning** — the carbon media is saturated and no longer adsorbing.
- **Visible sediment or cloudiness at fixtures** — the physical barrier has failed or is bypassing.
- **Discoloration on the cartridge** — if you have a clear sump housing, a brown or orange cartridge is done.
- **Appliance complaints** — scale or sediment showing up in ice makers, coffee machines, or the water heater fed by an overdue filter.

Act on any one of these. Waiting for two or three to stack up means you've been drinking unfiltered water for weeks.

## Tank systems vs cartridge systems: the maintenance gap

This is the biggest fork in the road, and it's worth understanding before you buy — not after.

**Cartridge systems** (like the [iSpring WGB32B](/filters/ispring-wgb32b/)) use replaceable cartridges in Big Blue housings. They're cheaper up front and genuinely DIY-friendly to service, but you're on a 6–12 month swap cycle forever, at roughly $100–$200 a year in cartridges.

**Tank systems** (like the [SpringWell CF1](/filters/springwell-cf1/)) hold a large bed of media that backwashes itself clean. The media is rated in the hundreds of thousands to a million gallons — years, not months. You still change the sediment pre-filter a couple of times a year, but the main event is nearly maintenance-free. The trade-off is a higher purchase price and a bigger footprint.

## How to change a cartridge (the 10-minute version)

1. Shut off water to the housing using the valves before and after it (this is why a bypass loop matters).
2. Press the red pressure-relief button on the housing cap.
3. Unscrew the sump with the housing wrench — have a bucket and towels ready, it's full of water.
4. Dump the old cartridge, rinse the sump, check the O-ring (a smear of silicone plumber's grease keeps it from seizing).
5. Drop in the new cartridge, hand-tighten the sump, then a quarter-turn with the wrench.
6. Turn the water back on slowly and check for leaks.

First-timers: watch for the O-ring. Nine out of ten post-change leaks are a pinched or dry O-ring, not a bad cartridge.

## What neglecting changes actually costs

An exhausted carbon filter isn't dangerous — it just stops working, so you're paying for protection you don't have. A clogged sediment filter is the expensive one: sustained pressure restriction stresses the housing, can crack sumps over time, and forces your well pump (if you have one) to work harder. Cartridges are $25–$60 each. A cracked housing or a burned-out pump is not.

Set a reminder, but trust the gauges. And if you're shopping for a system where this chore barely exists, start with our [2026 roundup](/posts/best-whole-house-water-filters-2026/) — the tank systems at the top are there largely because of how little they ask of you.

## What replacement cartridges actually cost

Knowing the schedule is half the battle; budgeting for it is the other half. Typical street prices for standard Big Blue (4.5" x 20") cartridges:

| Cartridge | Typical price | Per-year cost at normal intervals |
|---|---|---|
| 5-micron sediment (polyspun) | $15–$30 | $30–$120 (2–4 changes/yr) |
| Carbon block 5-micron | $40–$80 | $40–$160 (1–2 changes/yr) |
| Specialty (KDF, lead-reduction) | $60–$120 | $60–$240 |

A 3-stage cartridge system therefore costs roughly $100–$250 a year in media for most households — more on well water, less on clean municipal supplies. Tank systems flip the math: the [SpringWell CF1](/filters/springwell-cf1/) runs about $35 a year (just the pre-filter), while a [Pelican PSE2000](/filters/pelican-pse2000/) owner budgets more like $200–$300. This is why the cheapest system to buy is rarely the cheapest to own — run both through the [cost calculator](/tools/cost-calculator/) before deciding.

## How to actually remember

The best schedule is the one you'll follow. What works:

- **Gauges over calendars.** A $15 pressure gauge on each side of the housing beats any reminder app — when the differential climbs, change the cartridge. This automatically adjusts for vacations, guests, and seasonal sediment.
- **Tie it to something.** Sediment check with daylight-saving clock changes; carbon swap with the same weekend you service the HVAC. Anchored habits survive; phone reminders get snoozed.
- **Keep one spare set on hand.** The number-one reason changes get delayed is not having the cartridge. Buy the next set when you install the current one.
- **Log the date.** A strip of masking tape on the housing with the install date written in marker is the lowest-tech, highest-reliability tracking system ever devised.

## Seasonal gotchas

A few things that surprise owners: spring runoff can spike sediment for well owners (check monthly March–May); municipal main breaks or hydrant flushing send slugs of sediment through city lines (if your water runs brown for an afternoon, check the pre-filter the next day); and houseguests or a new baby quietly double your water volume, pulling every interval forward. The gauge catches all of this. The calendar catches none of it.
